## Analysis {#sec:analysis}

This section interprets the results of <<ref('sec:results')>>. Each subsection states what was observed, gives the most plausible explanation and, where the experiment cannot establish the explanation, says so. Differences of a few tenths of a percentage point are discussed only when a significance test supports them: the standard deviation of the ten fold accuracies of the best digit classifiers is about <<pct(cell('digits','hog+lbp','SVM-RBF','accuracy_std'), 1)>> points, and one wrong image changes the accuracy of the digit data by only <<pct(1/1797, 2)>> points.

### Feature extraction

**The descriptor matters most where the raw representation is a poor one.** For the digit images the effect of the descriptor on clean accuracy is modest, because even raw pixels are a good representation: the best raw-pixel classifier (<<bpb.loc[('digits', 'raw'), 'classifier']>>) reaches <<pct(bpb.loc[('digits', 'raw'), 'accuracy'])>>\%, whereas HOG gives <<pct(bpb.loc[('digits', 'hog'), 'accuracy'])>>\% with <<bpb.loc[('digits', 'hog'), 'classifier']>> and the complete hand-crafted descriptor <<pct(bpb.loc[('digits', 'handcrafted'), 'accuracy'])>>\%. Measured as an error rate, however, the improvement from raw pixels to HOG with the same RBF-SVM is from <<pct(1 - aD.loc['SVM-RBF', 'raw'])>>\% to <<pct(1 - aD.loc['SVM-RBF', 'hog'])>>\%, a reduction by a factor of <<'%.1f'|format((1 - aD.loc['SVM-RBF', 'raw']) / (1 - aD.loc['SVM-RBF', 'hog']))>>. The low-dimensional shape and texture descriptors are much weaker when used alone: Hu moments reach only <<pct(bpb.loc[('digits', 'hu'), 'accuracy'])>>\% and GLCM statistics <<pct(bpb.loc[('digits', 'glcm'), 'accuracy'])>>\%, because seven invariants or twelve co-occurrence statistics cannot encode the stroke geometry that separates ten classes. The 16-dimensional zoning descriptor, in contrast, keeps <<pct(bpb.loc[('digits', 'zoning'), 'accuracy'])>>\% accuracy with 16 numbers, which shows how much of the discriminative information of a digit is its coarse spatial layout of ink.

**For signals the descriptor decides everything.** On the waveform data the raw samples are almost useless for most models: the best raw-sample classifier (<<bpb.loc[('signals', 'raw'), 'classifier']>>) reaches <<pct(bpb.loc[('signals', 'raw'), 'accuracy'])>>\%, and the linear models (LDA, linear SVM, softmax) stay at <<pct(aS.loc['SVM-Linear', 'raw'])>>--<<pct(aS.loc['Bayes-LDA', 'raw'])>>\%, essentially the chance level of <<pct(1/6)>>\% for six classes. The reason is that the random phase and frequency of every waveform make the time-domain classes overlap completely: no linear combination of samples is a stable class indicator. The hand-crafted descriptor lifts the same models to <<pct(aS.loc['Bayes-LDA', 'handcrafted'])>>--<<pct(aS.loc['SVM-RBF', 'handcrafted'])>>\%. Within the descriptor, the spectral family is responsible for most of the gain (<<pct(bpb.loc[('signals', 'spectral'), 'accuracy'])>>\% from 16 spectral features against <<pct(bpb.loc[('signals', 'time'), 'accuracy'])>>\% from nine time-domain statistics), and adding the three Hjorth parameters to the time-domain statistics improves the best accuracy from <<pct(bpb.loc[('signals', 'time'), 'accuracy'])>>\% to <<pct(bpb.loc[('signals', 'time+hjorth'), 'accuracy'])>>\%. The strongest single feature of the ANOVA ranking is `<<st['signals_handcrafted']['top_feature']>>` (an auto-correlation feature, $F=<<'%.0f'|format(st['signals_handcrafted']['top_F'])>>$), i.e. a quantity that is invariant to phase, which is consistent with the explanation above.

**Separability criteria predict accuracy.** The trace criterion $J=\mathrm{tr}(S_W^{-1}S_B)$ of <<ref('tab:sep')>> orders the feature spaces almost in the same way as the best cross-validated accuracy: the Spearman rank correlation between $J$ and the accuracy is 0.93 over the eight digit feature spaces and 1.00 over the five signal feature spaces. A scatter-matrix criterion is therefore a cheap screening tool before any classifier is trained. It is not perfect: the 12 GLCM features have a ratio $\mathrm{tr}S_B/\mathrm{tr}S_W$ of <<'%.2f'|format(csv('separability_by_featureset.csv').set_index(['dataset', 'feature_set']).loc[('digits', 'glcm'), 'trace_Sb_over_trace_Sw'])>>, comparable to HOG, because the *trace* is dominated by a few high-variance directions, yet their classification accuracy is only <<pct(bpb.loc[('digits', 'glcm'), 'accuracy'])>>\%.

**Effective dimensionality is small.** The 256 raw pixels need <<S['pca_raw_components_95']>> principal components for 95\% and <<S['pca_raw_components_99']>> for 99\% of the variance, and the PCA sweep of <<ref('fig:dimsel_row')>> shows that 20 components already give 99.3\% with $k$-NN and 30 components 99.5\% with the SVM; beyond 50 components nothing is gained. For the full hand-crafted descriptor the 617 features have an effective rank (exponential of the spectral entropy) of only <<'%.1f'|format(st['digits_handcrafted']['effective_rank'])>>. Supervised filter selection exploits this redundancy (<<ref('fig:dimsel_row')>>): with only five features mRMR keeps <<pct(csv('sweep_feature_selection.csv').set_index('k').loc[5, 'mRMR'])>>\% accuracy against <<pct(csv('sweep_feature_selection.csv').set_index('k').loc[5, 'Fisher'])>>\% for the Fisher score and <<pct(csv('sweep_feature_selection.csv').set_index('k').loc[5, 'Mutual information'])>>\% for mutual information, because mRMR penalises redundancy while the other two rank features individually and therefore select several near-copies of the same good feature. With 160 or more features all three criteria are within one point of each other. Of the first 40 features selected by mRMR, 27 are HOG, 10 are projection profiles, two are GLCM and one is a Hu invariant; none is an LBP feature, in agreement with the ablation below.

### Statistical analysis

**The assumptions of the classical models are violated, and it hardly matters.** Only <<'%.1f'|format(100 * st['digits_handcrafted']['frac_normal_shapiro'])>>\% of the digit features pass the Shapiro--Wilk test, none of the signal features and <<'%.1f'|format(100 * st['wine_raw']['frac_normal_shapiro'])>>\% of the wine features; Levene's test rejects equal variances across classes for <<'%.1f'|format(100 * st['digits_handcrafted']['frac_levene_het'])>>\% of the digit features. Strictly, therefore, neither the normality assumption of Bayes classifiers nor the equal-covariance assumption of LDA holds. Nevertheless, Bayes-LDA reaches <<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-LDA'))>>\% on HOG+LBP, within <<'%.1f'|format(100 * (cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'SVM-RBF') - cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-LDA')))>> points of the best model, and the best classifier of the wine data is LDA (<<pct(bpb.loc[('wine', 'raw'), 'accuracy'])>>\%). The explanation is that a linear boundary estimated from first and second moments remains a good boundary when the classes are nearly separable, whether or not the densities are Gaussian. A violated assumption is a warning about *probability estimates* (see the calibration discussion below) rather than a prediction of poor accuracy.

**Multicollinearity makes covariance matrices singular.** The condition number of the digit-descriptor correlation matrix is $<<'%.1e'|format(st['digits_handcrafted']['condition_number'])>>$, <<st['digits_handcrafted']['pairs_corr_gt_0.9']>> feature pairs have $|r|>0.9$, and the effective rank is about <<'%.0f'|format(st['digits_handcrafted']['effective_rank'])>> although the matrix has 617 columns. Any method that inverts the sample covariance matrix must regularise it. The shrinkage sweep (<<ref('fig:sweep_row2')>>) shows the effect directly: the accuracy of the quadratic discriminant rises from <<pct(csv('sweep_bayes_reg.csv').set_index('shrinkage').loc[0.0, 'qda'])>>\% without shrinkage to <<pct(csv('sweep_bayes_reg.csv').set_index('shrinkage').loc[0.9, 'qda'])>>\% with a shrinkage of 0.9, and that of the naive Bayes classifier from <<pct(csv('sweep_bayes_reg.csv').set_index('shrinkage').loc[0.0, 'naive'])>>\% to <<pct(csv('sweep_bayes_reg.csv').set_index('shrinkage').loc[0.9, 'naive'])>>\%, whereas LDA, which pools the data of all classes, is almost insensitive (best at shrinkage 0.3). The collapse of the $k$-NN classifier with the Mahalanobis metric (<<pct(csv('sweep_knn.csv').set_index('k').loc[1, 'mahalanobis'])>>\% for $k=1$, falling to <<pct(csv('sweep_knn.csv').set_index('k').loc[25, 'mahalanobis'])>>\% for $k=25$, against more than 97\% for the Euclidean and Manhattan metrics) is consistent with the same cause: the pseudo-inverse of a nearly singular covariance matrix amplifies directions that contain only noise.

**Significance is not importance.** After Benjamini--Hochberg correction <<st['digits_handcrafted']['n_sig_bh_5pct']>> of the <<st['digits_handcrafted']['n_tested']>> digit features differ significantly between the classes, so the $p$-values cannot tell the useful features from the rest; the effect sizes can: the median $\eta^2$ is <<'%.2f'|format(st['digits_handcrafted']['median_eta2'])>> and the largest, for `<<st['digits_handcrafted']['top_feature']>>`, is <<'%.2f'|format(st['digits_handcrafted']['max_eta2'])>>. Across the descriptor families (<<ref('tab:famef')>>) the GLCM and projection-profile features have the largest *average* effect size, yet GLCM alone classifies poorly and HOG, whose average effect size is lower, carries almost all the information (see the ablation). Univariate effect size measures how much a single feature separates the classes; it does not measure complementarity, which is why multivariate criteria such as $J$ and mRMR are needed.

**Outliers and difficult pairs.** The Mahalanobis analysis flags <<st['digits_handcrafted']['n_mahalanobis_outliers']>> of the 1797 digit patterns but <<st['signals_handcrafted']['n_mahalanobis_outliers']>> of the 1200 signals, and <<st['breast_cancer_raw']['n_mahalanobis_outliers']>> of the 569 breast-cancer cases: the heavy-tailed feature distributions of the signals and of the medical measurements produce many large Mahalanobis distances although the observations are not errors. The pair of digit classes with the smallest Bhattacharyya distance is <<st['digits_handcrafted']['hardest_pair']>>; the per-class report (<<ref('tab:perclass')>>) confirms that class 9 has the lowest F1-score (<<'%.3f'|format(csv('per_class_report_digits_best.csv').set_index('class').loc['9', 'f1-score'])>>). For the signals the hardest pair is <<st['signals_handcrafted']['hardest_pair']>>, which is plausible because an amplitude-modulated tone is a sine wave with a slowly varying envelope. The statistical analysis therefore predicts where the classifier will fail before any classifier is trained.

### Classification

**A small group of models is best, and the Nemenyi test says they cannot be separated.** The Friedman test rejects the hypothesis that the <<S['friedman']['n_classifiers']>> classifiers perform equally ($\chi^2=<<'%.1f'|format(S['friedman']['chi2'])>>$, $p=<<'%.1e'|format(S['friedman']['p'])>>$). The RBF-SVM has the best average rank (<<'%.2f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['SVM-RBF'])>>), followed by the extremely randomised trees (<<'%.2f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['ExtraTrees'])>>), logistic regression (<<'%.2f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['LogReg(sk)'])>>), the MLP (<<'%.2f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['MLP'])>>) and the linear SVM (<<'%.2f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['SVM-Linear'])>>). With the critical difference of <<'%.2f'|format(S['friedman']['cd'])>> ranks, the SVM is significantly better than the <<(S['friedman']['avg_ranks'].items()|selectattr(1, 'gt', S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['SVM-RBF'] + S['friedman']['cd'])|list|length)>> classifiers whose average rank exceeds <<'%.2f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['SVM-RBF'] + S['friedman']['cd'])>> (<<(S['friedman']['avg_ranks'].items()|selectattr(1, 'gt', S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['SVM-RBF'] + S['friedman']['cd'])|map(attribute=0)|join(', '))>>), but it is *not* significantly better than the other <<S['friedman']['n_classifiers'] - 1 - (S['friedman']['avg_ranks'].items()|selectattr(1, 'gt', S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['SVM-RBF'] + S['friedman']['cd'])|list|length)>> classifiers, among them the from-scratch Bayes-LDA, softmax regression and MLP. The pairwise tests on the digit data (<<ref('tab:mcnemar')>>) tell the same story at a finer level: the RBF-SVM, the linear SVM and logistic regression make statistically indistinguishable errors (McNemar $p=<<'%.2f'|format(csv('significance_tests_digits.csv').set_index('B').loc['SVM-Linear', 'McNemar_p'])>>$ and $<<'%.2f'|format(csv('significance_tests_digits.csv').set_index('B').loc['LogReg(sk)', 'McNemar_p'])>>$), whereas the advantage over the MLP, the quadratic discriminant and the decision tree is significant under both the McNemar test and the corrected resampled $t$-test. The two tests do not always agree in strength (for the quadratic discriminant McNemar gives $p<0.001$ and the corrected $t$-test $p=<<'%.3f'|format(csv('significance_tests_digits.csv').set_index('B').loc['Bayes-QDA', 'corrected_t_p'])>>$) because the McNemar test treats the pooled out-of-fold predictions as independent and is therefore the more liberal of the two.

**From-scratch implementations are competitive.** Among the models implemented in this project, $k$-NN (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'kNN'))>>\%), Bayes-LDA (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-LDA'))>>\%), the MLP (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'MLP'))>>\%) and Bayes-QDA (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-QDA'))>>\%) are within one to 1.5 points of the best library model on HOG+LBP. Over all fifteen blocks the from-scratch Bayes-LDA, softmax regression and MLP are statistically indistinguishable from the best model, whereas $k$-NN (average rank <<'%.1f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['kNN'])>>) and Bayes-QDA (<<'%.1f'|format(S['friedman']['avg_ranks']['Bayes-QDA'])>>) lie just beyond the critical difference. One component is visibly weaker than its library counterpart: the from-scratch softmax regression reaches <<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Softmax'))>>\% whereas scikit-learn's logistic regression, which optimises the same model with a second-order solver until convergence, reaches <<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'LogReg(sk)'))>>\%. The gap is due to the optimiser (a fixed number of Adam epochs without tuning of the learning rate and of the regularisation), not to the model, and it is the kind of difference that only a side-by-side benchmark exposes.

**Why some models fail.** The weakest models are the minimum-distance classifier (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'MinDist'))>>\%), naive Bayes (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-Naive'))>>\%), the Parzen-window classifier (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Parzen'))>>\%) and the decision tree (<<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'DecisionTree'))>>\%). Each failure has a different cause that is visible in the earlier analysis. The minimum-distance classifier summarises each class by one point and ignores its covariance; naive Bayes assumes independent features although <<st['digits_handcrafted']['pairs_corr_gt_0.9']>> feature pairs are almost perfectly correlated; the Parzen classifier uses a single isotropic bandwidth in a space with 560 dimensions but an effective rank of about ten, so that the kernel is far too wide in the directions that carry no information; and a single tree partitions the space with axis-parallel cuts on individual features, has high variance, and loses <<'%.1f'|format(100 * (cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'RandomForest') - cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'DecisionTree')))>> points relative to a random forest built from many such trees.

**Accuracy is not the whole picture: calibration and cost.** The models with the lowest expected calibration error on HOG+LBP are Bayes-LDA (<<'%.3f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-LDA', 'ece'))>>), logistic regression (<<'%.3f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'LogReg(sk)', 'ece'))>>) and the MLP (<<'%.3f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'MLP', 'ece'))>>), i.e. the models that are trained, or derived, as probabilistic models. The SVM, whose probabilities are obtained by Platt scaling, has an ECE of <<'%.3f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'SVM-RBF', 'ece'))>>; the tree ensembles have <<'%.2f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'ExtraTrees', 'ece'))>> (random forest <<'%.2f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'RandomForest', 'ece'))>>), which given an accuracy above 98\% means that their votes are strongly under-confident, and the Parzen classifier reaches <<'%.2f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Parzen', 'ece'))>>, i.e. its posterior estimates are unusable as probabilities although its accuracy is <<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Parzen'))>>\%. An application that thresholds the confidence (for example, to reject uncertain digits) should therefore choose a calibrated model even at the price of a small loss of accuracy. The cost side is shown in <<ref('fig:time_vs_accuracy')>>: $k$-NN needs <<'%.3f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'kNN', 'fit_time'))>> s and Bayes-LDA <<'%.2f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-LDA', 'fit_time'))>> s per fold for a fit, against <<'%.1f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'SVM-RBF', 'fit_time'))>> s for the RBF-SVM and <<'%.1f'|format(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'RandomForest', 'fit_time'))>> s for the random forest, while giving <<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'kNN'))>>\% and <<pct(cell('digits', 'hog+lbp', 'Bayes-LDA'))>>\% accuracy; the cost of $k$-NN moves from training to prediction. (Timings were measured on two shared CPU cores and are indicative only.)

**Other data sets.** On the 178-sample wine data LDA is best (<<pct(bpb.loc[('wine', 'raw'), 'accuracy'])>>\%) and on the breast-cancer data logistic regression (<<pct(bpb.loc[('breast_cancer', 'raw'), 'accuracy'])>>\%): both are small, low-dimensional problems with nearly linear class boundaries, where the models with the strongest assumptions and the fewest parameters win. On the signal data the best accuracy on the full descriptor is <<pct(bpb.loc[('signals', 'handcrafted'), 'accuracy'])>>\% and <<(aS['handcrafted'] >= aS['handcrafted'].max() - 0.01).sum()>> of the <<aS['handcrafted'].count()>> classifiers lie within one point of the best.

### Hyper-parameters, sample efficiency and capacity

**Standardised features make the SVM insensitive to its hyper-parameters.** The accuracy surface of <<ref('fig:sweep_row1')>> has a broad plateau at about <<pct(csv('sweep_svm.csv').set_index('index').loc['C=10', 'gamma=0.3/d'])>>\% for $C\ge10$ and $\gamma/d\in[0.1,1]$; only very strong regularisation ($C=0.1$, almost no learning) or a very narrow kernel ($\gamma=3/d$, <<pct(csv('sweep_svm.csv').set_index('index').loc['C=10', 'gamma=3.0/d'])>>\%, over-fitting) are harmful. For $k$-NN the Euclidean and Manhattan metrics give nearly the same curve and the best neighbourhood is the smallest one (Manhattan, $k=1$: <<pct(csv('sweep_knn.csv').set_index('k').loc[1, 'manhattan'])>>\%); the accuracy declines slowly with $k$ (to <<pct(csv('sweep_knn.csv').set_index('k').loc[25, 'euclidean'])>>\% at $k=25$) because the digit classes are compact and nearly noise-free, so that averaging over more neighbours mainly adds neighbours from other classes.

**Capacity is not the bottleneck.** The six MLP architectures of <<ref('tab:mlparch')>>, from 9 thousand to 82 thousand parameters, reach test accuracies between <<pct(csv('sweep_mlp_architecture.csv').test_acc.min())>>\% and <<pct(csv('sweep_mlp_architecture.csv').test_acc.max())>>\%. With a test set of 450 images a difference of 0.5 points is two images, so the experiment cannot rank the architectures, and it shows that a single hidden layer of 16 units already suffices for HOG+LBP features. Early stopping ended training after 21--51 epochs, so the networks are large in relation to the 1347 training images and begin to over-fit within a few dozen epochs.

**Strong inductive bias wins with little data.** The learning curves (<<ref('fig:learning_curves')>>) show a cross-over: with 5\% of the training data (about 72 images) the minimum-distance classifier is the best of the five models (<<pct(csv('learning_curves.csv').set_index('train_fraction').loc[0.05, 'MinDist'])>>\% against <<pct(csv('learning_curves.csv').set_index('train_fraction').loc[0.05, 'SVM-RBF'])>>\% for the SVM), whereas from 20\% of the data (about 290 images) the SVM leads (the two are level at 10\%), and the minimum-distance classifier saturates at <<pct(csv('learning_curves.csv').set_index('train_fraction').loc[1.0, 'MinDist'])>>\% because its high bias cannot be reduced by more data. The curves of the SVM, $k$-NN and LDA are still rising slowly at the full training-set size, although by less than 0.3 points over the last quarter of the data.

### Descriptor ablation and robustness

**HOG carries the information; the other families are redundant on clean data.** In the ablation (<<ref('fig:ablation_groups')>> and <<ref('app:extra')>>) removing HOG is the only intervention that hurts noticeably: the $k$-NN accuracy falls from <<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='all groups' and classifier=='kNN'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\% to <<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='without hog' and classifier=='kNN'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\%, and the SVM from <<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='all groups' and classifier=='SVM-RBF'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\% to <<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='without hog' and classifier=='SVM-RBF'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\% (LDA loses a similar amount). Removing any other single family changes the accuracy by less than the noise level, and HOG alone (<<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='only hog' and classifier=='SVM-RBF'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\% with the SVM) is as good as the complete 617-dimensional descriptor. LBP alone is also strong (<<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='only lbp' and classifier=='SVM-RBF'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\%) and GLCM, Hu moments and the intensity statistics are weak alone (<<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='only glcm' and classifier=='SVM-RBF'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\%, <<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='only hu' and classifier=='SVM-RBF'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\% and <<pct(csv('ablation_descriptor_groups.csv').query("setting=='only stats' and classifier=='SVM-RBF'").accuracy.iloc[0])>>\%).

**On corrupted images the descriptors separate sharply.** Raw pixels are brittle (<<ref('fig:rob_row1')>>, <<ref('fig:rob_row2')>>): with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.1 the raw-pixel accuracy falls from 97.0\% to 21.6\%, a one-pixel horizontal shift reduces it to 58.8\% and a rotation by 10° to 64.4\%, whereas HOG+LBP keeps 97.7\% after a one-pixel shift and 96.5\% after a 10° rotation. Averaged over the severity levels and four classifiers, the ranking of the descriptors is HOG+LBP (<<pct(S['robustness_mean_by_featureset']['hog+lbp'])>>\%), HOG (<<pct(S['robustness_mean_by_featureset']['hog'])>>\%), LBP (<<pct(S['robustness_mean_by_featureset']['lbp'])>>\%) and raw pixels (<<pct(S['robustness_mean_by_featureset']['raw'])>>\%). The advantage of gradient histograms is plausibly due to their construction: pooling orientations over cells makes the descriptor tolerant to small shifts and rotations, and the block normalisation removes sensitivity to contrast (this explanation was not isolated by a separate experiment). There is one exception that the combined descriptor hides: for additive Gaussian noise HOG alone (<<pct(csv('robustness_summary.csv').set_index('corruption').loc['gaussian_noise', 'hog'])>>\%) is much better than HOG+LBP (<<pct(csv('robustness_summary.csv').set_index('corruption').loc['gaussian_noise', 'hog+lbp'])>>\%), since LBP compares individual pixel values with their neighbours and noise flips these comparisons. Concatenating descriptors is therefore not free: it adds the weaknesses of each part to the strengths. All robustness results are for models trained on clean images; training with augmented data would change the absolute numbers, though not the mechanism that makes HOG tolerant.

### Clustering

**Representation matters even more without labels.** In the HOG+LBP space reduced to 30 dimensions, Ward's agglomerative clustering recovers the digit classes best (ARI <<'%.3f'|format(S['clustering_best_main']['ARI'])>>, accuracy after optimal label matching <<pct(S['clustering_best_main']['cluster_acc'])>>\%), followed by spectral clustering, fuzzy $c$-means and $k$-means (<<ref('tab:clumain')>>). In the PCA space of the raw pixels the same methods are much worse (for $k$-means the ARI drops from <<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='KMeans (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])>> to <<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='raw -> PCA30' and algorithm=='KMeans (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])>>), a much larger penalty than the one a supervised classifier pays for raw pixels. A supervised projection (Fisher LDA to nine dimensions) lifts $k$-means to an ARI of <<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> LDA9 (supervised bound)' and algorithm=='KMeans (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])>>, which indicates how much of the gap to a perfect clustering is due to the representation and how much to the algorithm.

**The from-scratch algorithms reproduce the library results.** The from-scratch $k$-means (ARI <<'%.3f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='KMeans (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])>>) and the scikit-learn implementation (<<'%.3f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='KMeans (sklearn)'").ARI.iloc[0])>>) differ by only <<'%.3f'|format((csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='KMeans (sklearn)'").ARI.iloc[0] - csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='KMeans (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])|abs)>> in ARI, which is the size of difference that different random initialisations produce; the same holds in the other four feature spaces (<<ref('app:extra')>>).

**Model-based clustering helps when the clusters are elliptical.** Gaussian mixtures with full covariance are the best method on the signal data (ARI <<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_other_datasets.csv').query("dataset=='signals' and algorithm=='GMM-full (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])>> against <<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_other_datasets.csv').query("dataset=='signals' and algorithm=='KMeans (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])>> for $k$-means) and on the wine data (<<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_other_datasets.csv').query("dataset=='wine' and algorithm=='GMM-full (scratch)'").ARI.iloc[0])>>), where the classes have different variances and correlated features; they are weaker on the digits, where the 30-dimensional space contains ten clusters with about 180 points each and the 465 free parameters of a full covariance matrix per component are hard to estimate. Likewise, in the dedicated covariance experiment (<<ref('tab:gmmcov')>>; one EM initialisation per setting, same feature space) the BIC and the ARI prefer the full covariance (BIC <<'%.0f'|format(csv('gmm_covariance_types.csv').set_index('covariance').loc['full', 'BIC'])>> against <<'%.0f'|format(csv('gmm_covariance_types.csv').set_index('covariance').loc['spherical', 'BIC'])>> for spherical components), even after the penalty for the additional parameters. The price of flexibility is stability: under bootstrap resampling the mean pairwise ARI of the Gaussian mixture is <<'%.2f'|format(csv('cluster_stability.csv').set_index('algorithm').loc['GMM-full (scratch)', 'mean_pairwise_ARI'])>> against <<'%.2f'|format(csv('cluster_stability.csv').set_index('algorithm').loc['KMeans (scratch)', 'mean_pairwise_ARI'])>> for $k$-means (<<ref('tab:stab')>>).

**Density-based methods fail on these data.** DBSCAN and HDBSCAN are the two worst methods on the digits (ARI <<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='DBSCAN'").ARI.iloc[0])>> and <<'%.2f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='HDBSCAN'").ARI.iloc[0])>>), and on the wine and signal data they find a single cluster or two (<<ref('app:extra')>>). A plausible reason is that the digit classes touch each other in the feature space, so that there is no density valley between them, and that in 30 dimensions distances concentrate, which makes a single global $\varepsilon$ difficult to choose: the best $\varepsilon$ of the sweep (<<ref('fig:fcm_row')>>) yields <<csv('dbscan_sweep.csv').sort_values('ARI').iloc[-1].n_clusters|int>> clusters and discards <<pct(csv('dbscan_sweep.csv').sort_values('ARI').iloc[-1].noise_fraction, 0)>>\% of the points as noise.

**Internal validity indices can mislead.** HDBSCAN has the highest silhouette coefficient of all methods in <<ref('tab:clumain')>> (<<'%.3f'|format(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='HDBSCAN'").silhouette.iloc[0])>>) but the lowest ARI, because it labels <<pct(csv('clustering_results.csv').query("space=='hog+lbp -> PCA30' and algorithm=='HDBSCAN'").noise_fraction.iloc[0], 0)>>\% of the points as noise and computes the silhouette on the easy remainder. The criteria for the number of clusters (<<ref('fig:k_selection')>>) are also not a safe guide: the silhouette, the BIC and the gap statistic all select the largest value of the search range, $k=<<S['k_selection']['best_silhouette_k']>>$, instead of the ten true classes. This suggests that the digit classes consist of several sub-groups (different writing styles), so that a finer partition is statistically better supported than the label partition, although this explanation was not tested further. The practical lesson is to combine internal indices with domain knowledge and with a stability analysis rather than to select the number of clusters from one criterion.

### Threats to validity and limitations

* **Easy data.** The digits are small, centred and clean, and the best classifiers make fewer than ten errors in 1797 images; differences between the top models are therefore within the resolution of the data, and the robustness study is the more informative part of the evaluation.
* **Synthetic signals.** The waveform data were generated by the authors, so conclusions about signal descriptors demonstrate the workbench, not the performance on real recordings.
* **Dependence between blocks.** The Friedman test assumes independent data sets, but eight of the fifteen blocks are different feature sets of the same digit data and five are feature sets of the same signal data; the reported $p$-values are therefore optimistic, and the critical difference is best read as a visual aid.
* **Tuning on the evaluation data.** The hyper-parameters of the zoo were fixed in advance, but the sweeps were evaluated on the same cross-validation folds as the benchmark; their optima are slightly optimistic, and the sweeps were therefore not used to select the benchmark models.
* **Training on clean data only.** The robustness results measure how a model trained on clean images behaves under corruption, not what an augmented training set could achieve.
* **Timing.** Run times were measured on two shared CPU cores and vary between runs.
* **Seeds.** All results use a single global seed; the fold-to-fold standard deviations quantify the resampling noise, but not the variation due to the data-generation seed.
