
# Literature Survey

This chapter reviews the body of work on which each stage of the workbench rests. The survey is organised along the stages of the pattern recognition cycle introduced in Chapter 1: feature extraction and dimensionality reduction, statistical analysis, classification, clustering, and performance evaluation. It closes with a comparison of existing software toolkits and a statement of the gap that this project addresses.

## Evolution of Statistical Pattern Recognition

The mathematical foundations of the field were laid long before the term "pattern recognition" was coined. Fisher's analysis of the iris measurements introduced the linear discriminant and the idea of a projection that maximises between-class relative to within-class scatter <<cite('fisher1936')>>; Pearson and Hotelling had earlier introduced principal components as the directions of maximal variance <<cite('pearson1901','hotelling1933')>>; Mahalanobis' generalised distance gave the correct way of measuring distances when features are correlated <<cite('mahalanobis1936')>>. The 1960s brought the non-parametric era: the nearest-neighbour rule and its asymptotic error bound of at most twice the Bayes error <<cite('cover1967')>>, and the kernel density estimator of Parzen <<cite('parzen1962')>>. Hughes showed that for a fixed training-set size the accuracy of a statistical recogniser first rises and then falls as the number of measurements grows <<cite('hughes1968')>>, the first formal statement of what Bellman had called the curse of dimensionality and what Beyer *et al.* later showed to undermine the meaning of "nearest" in high dimensions <<cite('beyer1999','aggarwal2001')>>.

The 1980s and 1990s were dominated by two developments. The back-propagation algorithm made multilayer perceptrons trainable <<cite('rumelhart1986')>>, and the support vector machine combined the maximum-margin principle with kernels <<cite('cortes1995')>>. LeCun's convolutional network applied to handwritten digits showed that feature extraction itself could be learned <<cite('lecun1998')>>, a line of research that culminated in the deep networks of the 2010s <<cite('krizhevsky2012','he2016')>>. Comprehensive reviews of the classical period are given by Jain, Duin and Mao <<cite('jain2000')>> and in the textbooks of Duda, Hart and Stork, Bishop, Theodoridis and Koutroumbas, Fukunaga, Hastie *et al.* and Murphy <<cite('duda2001','bishop2006','theodoridis2009','fukunaga1990','hastie2009','murphy2012')>>.

Although deep learning now dominates large-scale perception, the classical pipeline has not become obsolete. It remains the method of choice when the data set is small, when the features must be interpretable, when computation is limited (embedded and instrumentation settings) and when a statistical guarantee about the behaviour of the system is required. It is also the conceptual vocabulary in which deep models are explained: a convolutional layer is a learned bank of oriented filters, exactly the kind of operator that HOG and Gabor descriptors fix by hand.

## Feature Extraction

### Descriptors for images

A good image descriptor discards nuisance variation (illumination, small shifts, noise) while keeping the information that separates the classes. The **Histogram of Oriented Gradients** of Dalal and Triggs partitions the image into small cells, accumulates a histogram of gradient orientations weighted by gradient magnitude in every cell, and normalises overlapping groups of cells (blocks) to obtain illumination invariance <<cite('dalal2005')>>. It was introduced for pedestrian detection but is equally effective for character recognition because strokes are, in essence, oriented edges. **Local Binary Patterns** encode the sign of the difference between a pixel and its circular neighbourhood as a binary code; Ojala *et al.* showed that the "uniform" patterns (at most two bit transitions) carry most of the texture information and that mapping to rotation-invariant uniform codes gives a compact, grey-scale and rotation invariant histogram <<cite('ojala2002')>>. **Grey-level co-occurrence matrices** record how often pairs of grey levels occur at a given displacement; Haralick's statistics (contrast, homogeneity, energy, entropy, correlation) summarise the matrix <<cite('haralick1973')>>. **Moment invariants** are functionals of the image intensity that remain unchanged under translation, scaling and rotation; Hu derived seven such combinations of normalised central moments <<cite('hu1962')>>. Local keypoint descriptors such as SIFT <<cite('lowe2004')>> extend the same ideas to scale-space but are designed for matching objects across views and are therefore not used for the small, aligned images of this project.

### Descriptors for signals

For one-dimensional signals the classical descriptors come from three domains. *Time-domain* statistics (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, crest factor, zero-crossing rate) describe the amplitude distribution; Hjorth's activity, mobility and complexity describe the variance of the signal and of its first two derivatives and were introduced for EEG analysis <<cite('hjorth1970')>>. *Frequency-domain* descriptors are computed from the power spectrum obtained with the fast Fourier transform <<cite('cooley1965')>>: spectral centroid, spread, entropy, flatness, roll-off, band powers and the energy at harmonics of the fundamental. The harmonic structure is what distinguishes a square wave (odd harmonics) from a sawtooth (all harmonics) when both have the same fundamental. *Auto-correlation* descriptors capture periodicity and decay without requiring a spectral peak to be identified.

### Dimensionality reduction and feature selection

High-dimensional descriptors are usually redundant. **Principal Component Analysis** finds the orthogonal directions of maximal variance and gives the best rank-$k$ linear reconstruction in the least-squares sense <<cite('pearson1901','hotelling1933')>>; it is unsupervised and may discard discriminative low-variance directions. **Fisher's linear discriminant** is supervised: for $C$ classes it yields at most $C-1$ directions that maximise class separation <<cite('fisher1936','fukunaga1990')>>. Non-linear embeddings such as t-SNE <<cite('vandermaaten2008')>> and UMAP <<cite('mcinnes2018')>> are valuable for visualisation but are not used as classifier front-ends here, because they do not provide a stable out-of-sample mapping.

Feature *selection* keeps a subset of the original variables, which preserves interpretability. Guyon and Elisseeff distinguish filters (rank features by a statistic independent of any classifier), wrappers (search the subset space using a classifier) and embedded methods (selection is part of training) <<cite('guyon2003')>>. The minimum-redundancy maximum-relevance criterion of Peng *et al.* is a filter that favours features that are informative about the class but not redundant with those already selected <<cite('peng2005')>>.

## Statistical Analysis of Feature Spaces

Before a model is trained, a feature space deserves to be examined statistically. The Shapiro--Wilk and D'Agostino--Pearson tests assess whether a feature is Gaussian, which matters because Gaussian class-conditional models assume it <<cite('shapiro1965','dagostino1973')>>. The one-way analysis of variance quantifies how strongly a feature separates the classes; its non-parametric counterpart is the Kruskal--Wallis test <<cite('kruskal1952')>>, and Levene's test checks the equality of variances that ANOVA assumes <<cite('levene1960')>>. Statistical significance is not practical importance: effect sizes such as $\eta^2$ express the *fraction* of variance explained by the class <<cite('cohen1988')>>. When hundreds of features are tested simultaneously, some will be "significant" by chance; the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure controls the *false-discovery rate* rather than the family-wise error and is more powerful than Bonferroni correction <<cite('benjamini1995')>>.

Multivariate questions are answered with the Mahalanobis distance, whose squared value follows a chi-square distribution for Gaussian data and therefore gives a principled outlier threshold <<cite('mahalanobis1936','anderson2003')>>. Class separability can be summarised by scatter-matrix criteria <<cite('fukunaga1990')>> or, more precisely, by the Bhattacharyya distance between the class-conditional densities, whose exponential bounds the Bayes error from above <<cite('bhattacharyya1943','fukunaga1990')>>.

## Classification

**Bayesian decision theory** is the theoretical reference: assigning each pattern to the class of maximum posterior probability minimises the error rate <<cite('duda2001')>>. With Gaussian class-conditional densities the rule is quadratic (QDA) and, if the class covariances are equal, linear (LDA). Estimating a full covariance matrix of a high-dimensional feature vector from a modest sample is ill-conditioned, and *shrinkage* of the sample covariance towards a structured target gives a well-conditioned estimator <<cite('ledoit2004')>>. **Non-parametric** rules avoid distributional assumptions: the $k$-nearest-neighbour rule <<cite('cover1967')>> and the Parzen-window estimator <<cite('parzen1962')>> converge to the Bayes rule as the sample grows but are expensive at prediction time and sensitive to the metric and the bandwidth.

**Linear and neural models** learn the decision function directly. Softmax regression is the multiclass generalisation of logistic regression; multilayer perceptrons add hidden layers trained by back-propagation <<cite('rumelhart1986')>> with adaptive step-size optimisers such as Adam <<cite('kingma2015')>>. **Support vector machines** maximise the margin and, through the kernel trick, produce non-linear boundaries <<cite('cortes1995')>>; as SVMs do not output probabilities, Platt scaling is used to calibrate their scores <<cite('platt1999')>>. **Ensembles** of decision trees (random forests <<cite('breiman2001')>>, extremely randomised trees <<cite('geurts2006')>>, gradient boosting <<cite('friedman2001')>>) are strong general-purpose baselines that require little tuning. Modern classifiers are frequently *mis-calibrated*: their confidence does not match their accuracy <<cite('guo2017')>>, a property that the workbench measures with the expected calibration error.

## Clustering

Clustering partitions unlabelled patterns into groups of similar objects <<cite('jaindubes1988','xu2005')>>. **Partitional** methods minimise a within-cluster criterion: Lloyd's algorithm for $k$-means alternates assignment and centroid update <<cite('lloyd1982')>>, and the $k$-means++ seeding of Arthur and Vassilvitskii yields an $O(\log k)$-competitive solution and avoids poor local optima <<cite('arthur2007')>>. **Model-based** clustering fits a mixture of Gaussians by expectation-maximisation <<cite('dempster1977')>> and selects the number of components with an information criterion such as the BIC <<cite('schwarz1978')>>. **Fuzzy** c-means allows each pattern to belong to every cluster with a degree of membership <<cite('bezdek1981')>>. **Hierarchical** agglomerative clustering builds a dendrogram; Ward's criterion merges the pair of clusters that least increases the total within-cluster variance <<cite('ward1963')>>. **Density-based** methods find clusters of arbitrary shape: DBSCAN grows clusters from dense core points and labels isolated points as noise <<cite('ester1996')>>, and HDBSCAN removes the need to fix a single density threshold <<cite('campello2013')>>. **Spectral** clustering embeds the data using eigenvectors of a graph Laplacian and then applies $k$-means <<cite('ng2001')>>.

Because there are no labels, *validating* a clustering is as important as producing it. Internal indices such as the silhouette coefficient <<cite('rousseeuw1987')>>, the Davies--Bouldin index <<cite('davies1979')>> and the Calinski--Harabasz index <<cite('calinski1974')>> measure compactness and separation; the gap statistic compares the within-cluster dispersion with that expected under a uniform reference distribution <<cite('tibshirani2001')>>. When reference labels exist (as in a benchmark) external indices are available: the adjusted Rand index <<cite('hubert1985')>>, normalised and adjusted mutual information <<cite('vinh2010')>>, and cluster accuracy computed after an optimal one-to-one matching of clusters to classes with the Hungarian algorithm <<cite('kuhn1955')>>.

## Performance Evaluation

Estimating how well a classifier will perform on future data requires a resampling protocol. Kohavi's study established stratified ten-fold cross-validation as a sound default for accuracy estimation <<cite('kohavi1995')>>, and repeating the partitioning reduces the variance of the estimate. The two classic sources of optimism are *leakage* (any statistic computed with test data, including a scaler or a feature selector fitted on the full data set) and *selection bias* (using the same cross-validation both to tune and to report) <<cite('cawley2010')>>. The workbench therefore refits every pre-processing step inside each training fold.

Accuracy alone is insufficient. Cohen's kappa and the Matthews correlation coefficient correct for chance agreement and class imbalance <<cite('cohen1960','matthews1975')>>; per-class precision, recall and F-measure, receiver operating characteristic curves and calibration diagnostics describe different aspects of behaviour <<cite('guo2017')>>. Uncertainty is quantified with bootstrap confidence intervals <<cite('efron1993')>>.

Finally, *differences* between classifiers must be tested. Dietterich analysed several tests and recommended McNemar's test for a single train/test split and the 5$\times$2 cross-validation $t$-test when training can be repeated <<cite('dietterich1998')>>; since the folds of cross-validation are not independent, Nadeau and Bengio proposed a *corrected* resampled $t$-test whose variance accounts for the overlap of training sets <<cite('nadeau2003')>>. For comparing many classifiers on many data sets, Demšar recommends the Friedman rank test <<cite('friedman1937')>> followed by the Nemenyi post-hoc test <<cite('nemenyi1963','demsar2006')>>, visualised in a critical-difference diagram. All of these procedures are implemented in the workbench.

The survey shows that every individual technique is well documented and available in some library, but that *studies* built on them frequently fall short in three respects: (i) the descriptors, the statistical examination of the feature space and the classifiers are produced by different tools and therefore rarely examined together; (ii) the algorithms are used as black boxes, so that the student never confronts the numerical details (covariance conditioning, log-domain arithmetic, empty clusters, initialisation) that decide whether they work; and (iii) the evaluation seldom goes beyond a single accuracy figure, ignoring calibration, uncertainty and significance. The present project closes this gap by providing one reproducible code base in which each stage is implemented from first principles, verified against trusted libraries, and evaluated with a rigorous protocol.
